Easy Lawn Care

By the Hollyhock Lane Editorial Team · Last reviewed: October 2026

A good lawn is less about hard work than about a few sensible habits. Here is how to mow, water, feed, and overseed for a healthy lawn — and gentler, low-water options if you would rather not fuss.

On this page

  1. Mow high
  2. Watering
  3. The seasonal rhythm
  4. Overseeding
  5. Low-water options

The single most important habit: mow high

If you change only one thing about your lawn care, make it this: raise the blade and mow high. Cutting grass too short is the most common mistake there is, and it causes most of the troubles that follow — weeds, bare patches, drought stress, and shallow roots.

Set your mower to leave the grass about three to four inches tall for most common lawn grasses. Taller grass shades out weed seeds before they can sprout, grows deeper roots that reach water in dry spells, and simply looks lush and healthy. A good rule is never to remove more than a third of the blade's height at a single mowing, even if that means cutting a little more often in the quick-growing weeks. Cutting too much at once shocks the grass and leaves it pale and struggling, so patience with the clippers is well repaid.

Tip: Keep your mower blade sharp. A dull blade tears the grass rather than cutting it cleanly, leaving ragged brown tips and an open door to disease. A sharpening once or twice a season makes a visible difference.

Watering deeply and seldom

As with garden plants, a lawn wants deep, infrequent watering rather than a daily sprinkle. The aim is to soak the soil several inches down, which encourages deep roots that can fend for themselves between waterings.

  1. Water early in the morning, before the heat, so little is lost and the grass dries by evening.
  2. Give about one inch of water a week, counting rainfall, in one or two good soakings.
  3. To measure an inch, set an empty tuna can on the lawn and stop when it fills.
  4. In the heat of summer, many lawns naturally go a little dormant and brown; this is normal, and they green up again with cooler, wetter weather.

The seasonal rhythm

A lawn has a year, and working with it is far easier than working against it.

In spring, rake away the winter's debris, give the first mow once the grass is actively growing, and tackle weeds while they are young. In summer, mow high, water deeply when needed, and let the lawn rest through the hottest spells rather than forcing it. In fall — the most important season of all for a lawn — overseed any thin spots, feed the soil, and keep mowing until growth stops. In winter, simply let it rest, and keep heavy foot traffic off frozen or snow-covered grass.

Overseeding thin spots

Overseeding — scattering fresh grass seed over an existing lawn — is the gentle way to thicken thin areas and crowd out weeds. Early fall is the ideal time in most of the country, when the soil is still warm but the air has cooled.

What you'll need

  • Grass seed suited to your region and sun conditions
  • A rake
  • A light topping of compost or fine soil
  • A way to keep the area gently watered for a few weeks
  1. Mow a little lower than usual and rake away debris so seed can reach the soil.
  2. Scatter seed evenly over the thin areas, following the rate on the bag.
  3. Rake lightly and add a thin topping of compost to hold moisture.
  4. Keep the seeded area consistently damp — a light daily sprinkle — until the new grass is established, then return to deep, occasional watering.

Tip: Choose a seed labeled for your light conditions — sun, shade, or a mix. Grass sown in the wrong light will struggle no matter how well you tend it.

Feeding the soil, not just the grass

A lawn, like a garden, grows from its soil, and the kindest way to feed it is to think of the soil first. The simplest, most natural feed of all costs nothing: leave the clippings on the lawn when you mow. Far from causing thatch, these fine clippings break down quickly and return their nitrogen to the soil, meaning a well-mown lawn feeds itself a good deal of what it needs.

Beyond that, a yearly topdressing of fine compost, raked lightly into the surface in fall, works wonders for a tired lawn, improving the soil and encouraging deep, healthy roots. If you choose to use a lawn fertilizer, fall is the most valuable time to apply one in most regions, and autumn feeding does more good than the spring feeding many people reach for out of habit. Always follow the bag's rate exactly and water it in; too much lawn fertilizer scorches the grass and runs off to pollute waterways, helping no one.

Tip: A soil test from your Cooperative Extension office, done once every few years, tells you exactly what your lawn needs — and often reveals you need far less fertilizer than you thought, saving money and effort.

Common lawn troubles, calmly met

Most lawn problems trace back to the same few causes, and most are gentler to fix than they appear. Bare patches usually come from heavy traffic, pets, or deep shade; loosen the soil, overseed, and keep the spot watered until it fills in. Weeds are nearly always a sign of a thin or stressed lawn, and the best weed control of all is a thick, healthy lawn mown high, which shades weed seeds before they can sprout.

Moss points to shade, poor drainage, or compacted, acidic soil; it is a symptom, not a disease, and improving the conditions helps more than scraping it away. Brown patches in summer are often just natural dormancy in the heat, and the grass greens up with cooler, wetter weather — no cause for alarm or extra watering. Reading these signs saves a great deal of needless worry and work.

A kinder approach to weeds

It is worth making peace with a few weeds. A lawn dotted with clover and a daisy or two is greener in drought, friendlier to bees, and perfectly pleasant to sit on — and clover even feeds the soil. Chasing the flawless, weed-free lawn of the advertisements often means more chemicals, more water, and more work than the result is worth.

Where you do want to remove a weed, the gentlest ways are often the best: pull dandelions and other deep-rooted weeds by hand after rain, when the soil is soft and the whole root comes away. For the rest, simply mowing high and keeping the lawn thick and healthy will crowd most weeds out over time. If you ever use a weedkiller, follow the label precisely, keep it off flowers and vegetables, and remember that your Cooperative Extension office can suggest the least-harmful approach for your region.

Aerating and relieving compaction

Over the years, a lawn's soil can grow compacted underfoot, squeezing out the air and space that roots need and leaving the grass thin and tired no matter how you feed and water it. The gentle cure is aeration — making small holes across the lawn to let air, water, and nutrients reach the roots again. It is one of the most useful and least-known lawn kindnesses.

For a small lawn, a simple hand aerator or even a garden fork pushed in and rocked gently will do; for a larger one, you can rent a core aerator for an afternoon. Early fall is the ideal time in most regions, often paired with overseeding and a topdressing of compost for a lawn-renewing trio. If your lawn feels hard underfoot, puddles after rain, or stays stubbornly thin in a well-trodden path, compaction is likely the culprit, and a yearly aeration works quiet wonders. There is no need to overdo it — once a year, or even every other year, is plenty for most lawns.

Gentler, low-water options

Not everyone wants a thirsty, high-maintenance lawn, and there is no need to feel you must. If watering and mowing have become a burden, or if your region is often dry, there are kinder alternatives worth considering.

  • Drought-tolerant grasses such as fine fescues need far less water and mowing than thirstier types.
  • Clover, mixed into or in place of grass, stays green with little water, feeds the soil, and needs rare mowing.
  • Ground covers and native plantings can replace a lawn entirely in difficult spots, with far less fuss once established.
  • Reducing the lawn's size — giving part of it over to beds, mulch, or a patio — is a perfectly good answer, too.

Your local Cooperative Extension office can recommend the grasses and ground covers best suited to your climate, which saves a great deal of trial and error. There is also a growing, cheerful movement toward lawns that work with nature rather than against it — a patch of clover for the bees, a corner left a little longer and wilder, a bed of tough native plants where grass always struggled. None of this is untidy; done with a little thought, it is beautiful, and it asks far less of your time, your water, and your back. A lawn should serve you, not the other way around, and a smaller, gentler one is no failure — it is good sense.
